\begin{abstract}
Huntington--Hill separates a publicly chosen apportionment rule from its
mechanical execution. We study the corresponding architecture for district
boundaries: a fixed floor/ceiling recursive tree reduces a $k$-district plan to
$k-1$ two-way geographic decisions. Historical tract-level experiments show
how recursive graph bisection behaves under one implementation profile; they do
not prove a uniquely fair map or nationwide exact optimality. The certified
extension freezes the unit universe, population, adjacency, objective order,
and canonical tie-break, then attaches independently checkable evidence to
each cut. A staged ceremony commits the rule at genesis and releases certified
cuts by tree depth after fixed review intervals, allowing public verification
of each disclosed prefix without access to future descendants. This turns the
Huntington--Hill analogy into a testable governance claim: institutions choose
the rule, while software proves and publicly records its execution. Partisan
effects, Voting Rights Act obligations, legal validity, and public legitimacy
remain separate questions.
\end{abstract}
