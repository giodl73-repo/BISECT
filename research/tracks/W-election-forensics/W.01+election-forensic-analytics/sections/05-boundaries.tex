\section{Boundaries}

Every W-series page must lead with the same boundary, and this overview fixes
it. Forensic analytics can prioritize investigation. They cannot prove fraud,
certify correctness, or replace risk-limiting audits. A high anomaly score is a
reason to look more closely, not a finding. The investigation-note and
false-positive boundary fields exist precisely so that a score is never
circulated without its caveat.

The separation from the V-series is structural, not just rhetorical. V-series
algorithms verify audit and canvass claims with explicit evidence contracts;
their transcripts are designed to support certification-style claims when their
assumptions hold. W-series analytics explore patterns that may motivate more
review; their transcripts are designed to support triage. RCOUNT must keep the
two transcript kinds distinct so that an anomaly report can never be presented,
stored, or audited as if it were certification evidence.

Two further boundaries follow. First, forensic analytics need clean public data;
their value is bounded by the quality and completeness of the normalized RCOUNT
inputs, and a sparse or biased baseline population can manufacture apparent
anomalies. Second, the analytics are descriptive: they identify patterns that
need explanation, and the explanation --- benign or otherwise --- is an external,
human judgment that the W-series deliberately does not make.
