\begin{abstract}
Congressional redistricting in the United States suffers a legitimacy crisis:
partisan mapmakers manipulate district boundaries to predetermine election outcomes,
eroding public confidence in representative democracy. We evaluate recursive graph
bisection---a computational method used for supercomputer workload distribution---as a
partisan-input-excluded geographic benchmark. The execution stage does not consume
partisan data, but structure, weights, resolution, seed, and legal modifications remain
public policy choices. Governed NRS v0.3 runs assign all 27,398,654 Census blocks
across the 2000, 2010, and 2020 cycles. Each cycle covers 50 States, 435 districts,
and 385 recursive nodes with independently verified assignment, connectivity, and
population-tolerance conformance. Exact weighted-boundary and canonical proof
coverage remains zero of 1,155 nodes. Historical demographic, compactness, and
partisan analyses belong to earlier tract-based profiles and are not outcome claims
for NRS v0.3. A separately governed 2020 common-block bakeoff now reports
assignment overlap, subdivision splits, and four block-projected geometry
metrics for NRS v0.3 and official CD118 assignments; it does not establish
original-linework compactness or plan superiority. The contribution is therefore a reproducible operational baseline and
research program, not proof of a uniquely fair map or a substitute for commissions,
courts, civil-rights analysis, or public judgment.
\end{abstract}
